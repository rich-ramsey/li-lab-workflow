Preregistration

There are many different ways to pre-register a study, such as a pre-registration vs a registered report. And there are different philosophies of how to approach pre-registration, in terms of the amount of depth and detail required. See the following link for a justification and an example of a minimalist approach to pre-registration. A minimalist approach is not always possible, but I do like the idea of stripping the experiment back to the key verifiable aspects, and leaving the justification for the introduction to the paper.

You should also ask yourself if you need to preregister the study based on the aims and objectives of the science. Preregistation has these benefits by design - it prevents p-hacking, HARKing, publication bias and places constraints on underpowered studies (Lakens et al., 2024; Scheel et al., 2021). But it is not magic. You could still pre-register a fundamentally flawed study, simply because you can pre-register anything.

I think the key point is that it adds an objective and verifiable aspects of transparency, which inks thinking before and after the data were collected. And in my experience over the last ~10 years or so working with PhD students and postdocs on pre-registrations, the biggest win is that it forces everyone to address concrete questions about the links between research questions, hypotheses, analyses, and inference BEFORE you collect the data. It moves the timeline and the type of conversation between team members from a more diffuse and abstract sense of this is the general research question, design and type of analysis to what EXACTLY will we do and how will we analyse the data to evaluate our hypotheses. So, in my experience, it is an extremely valuable process to go through as a scientific team.

But not all science needs to be pre-registered. This should be an obvious point. If you are trying to generate hypotheses to test in the future then there may well be no need to pre-register anything. Likewise, if you are working from a computational or mathematical model of a system, which is in the public domain, and which generates explicit hypotheses, then you could simply refer to that model as the foundation for your hypotheses. Alternatively, maybe the approach to science that you are using is an iterative, model building exercise, which emerges more organically and cannot be boiled down to a snapshot of questions and hypotheses. This is all fine.

I would just say that for the vast majority of contemporary research in psychological and brain science, as well as related disciplines, preregistrations can and should be a sensible default expectation. But this statement probably says as much about the dominant monoculture of modern psychological and brain science, rather than the universal need for preregistration. In other words, I think science would be far better served by a broader and more diverse research base. But until then, preregistration will probably serve a useful purpose for many active researchers.

And you can pre-register exploratory work also, and this is not a contradiction. It is not necessary to pre-register exploratory work, but it could have advantages. As one example, if you have a question in mind, and you plan to access a large secondary dataset to address the question, it might be worth pre-registering your intent, if not an exact and specific analysis pipeline. This might be that of the 2000 variables available in the dataset, you only have interest in 12 to address your stated research question.

Here is a video of James explaining the process. The general approach would be to setup a quarto template doc, and then for each new study, complete the sections, generate a pdf and then submit that to your preferred pre-registration site. Then keep those documents in your project folder too. The template below is largely left empty at this stage, just to show the process.

21. Inference criteria

We will evaluate the contrast effects based on the posterior distributions using three complementary criteria: sign, size, and precision. First, we will assess whether the credible interval excludes zero. Specifically, we will report whether the 90% credible interval lies entirely above, entirely below, or includes zero. This addresses the sign (or direction) of the effect. Second, we will examine the size or magnitude of the posterior median. Third, we will assess the width of the 90% credible interval as an indicator of estimation precision. Narrow intervals indicate precise estimates, while wide intervals indicate substantial uncertainty that may warrant cautious interpretation or additional data collection.

We will report all three criteria together to provide a complete characterization of our findings (Cumming, 2014; Frank et al., 2025; Kruschke & Liddell, 2018; McElreath, 2020). For example, a positive median (sign) with large magnitude (size) but wide credible interval (precision) would be interpreted as suggestive but inconclusive evidence, whereas the same sign and size with a narrow interval excluding zero would constitute stronger evidence for an effect. This approach ensures we avoid over-interpreting imprecise estimates and adequately characterize the strength of evidence in our data.
