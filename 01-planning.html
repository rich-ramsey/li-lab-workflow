Based on my conversations with various people in the learning and instruction lab, as well as many folks in other psychology and human neuroscience labs, there is a discrepancy between how people say they plan studies (or how they would like to plan studies) and how they plan studies in practice. Most people try to concoct an a-prior power analysis, since that seems to be deemed more acceptable in mainstream journals and general discourse. But scratch beneath the surface and the most common approach in practice is the “resource constraints” approach. This is not a criticism, it is simply an observation.

The resource constraints approach targets the maximum reasonable sample size possible based on the resources available. This resource-based approach lends itself to planning via determining the minimal statistically detectable effect, or a sensitivity power analysis (rather than an a-priori power analysis; see Table 1.2). Using resource constraints to determine effect size sensitivity would be a lot simpler than the simulation-based approaches outlined below, as it determines the size of an effect that one could detect with a specified research design and level of statistical power. So, before you get lost digging into the weeds below, I would strongly suggest that you have a conversation with your research group about implications of a resource constraints approach within the context of your project. A resource constraints approach will save you a lot of time, and in the majority of cases it will more straightforwardly embody how your sample size is being set in practice.

And for reasonably simple designs and analyses that avoid more complex, multi-level modelling approaches, then a tool like G*Power can be very useful. And even if you plan on fitting a multi-level model, G*Power can be useful to give you a quick-and-dirty ballpark estimate based on a simplified aspect of your research design. So, I would recommend becoming familiar with G*Power.

But, if you want to plan a study that does try to capture the nested or multi-level nature of the data, such as by allowing for variation across participants or measurement items (Barr et al., 2013), then things get complicated fast. And we need to harness the power of data simulation. So, if you’re a sucker for punishment, and if you want a more involved and simulated-based approach, then continue reading.

The approach is primarily based on two resources: a series of blog posts by Solomon Kurz, and an R package called {faux} by Lisa Debruine. There are also many other simulation-based approaches to plan studies with multi-level models - see the bottom of this link for details. And more generally, I would strongly recommend using data simulation more generally for a variety of reasons throughout the scientific workflow that extend beyond planning (DeBruine & Barr, 2021; Gelman, 2024). See this talk by Lisa DeBruine for a nice summary.

The basic approach that I follow here is as follows:

  1. Find existing data.
  2. Fit a model to the data.
  3. Use the model to guide expectations on multi-level structure.
  4. Use simulations to vary parameters and guide sample size decisions.

3. Fit a model

Fit a “maximal” model as specified by the design.

Maximal varying effects (varying effects are “random” effects in frequentist language) model that is specified by the design. See this paper by Barr et al., (2013) for a clear justification of taking the “maximal” approach: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749596X12001180

To help develop understanding, I will build up to the maximal model here by showing the formulas for two other models first.

  1. A single level model formula. formula <- bf(correct ~ group + pre_score_z)

  2. Add varying intercepts by pid. formula <- bf(correct ~ group + pre_score_z + (1 | pid))

  3. Add varying intercepts and slopes by item_id. formula <- bf(correct ~ group + pre_score_z + (1 | pid), (1 + group | item_id))

4. Understanding effect sizes under the logit link

This section is hidden to streamline and simplify the chapter. Click “Show section” to read more on understanding effect sizes in logisitic regression.

Show section

Before running simulations, it is worth establishing a clear mapping between the model’s native parameterisation and effect sizes that are interpretable to a general audience. This section documents that mapping as a reference point for collaborators and for later simulation design.

The scale problem

Model fit_b1 uses a Bernoulli likelihood with the logit link, so all regression coefficients live on the log-odds scale. The posterior estimate for groupembodied is 0.15 (95% CI: −0.37, 0.68). This means that being in the embodied group increases the log-odds of a correct response by 0.15 relative to control — a number that is essentially uninterpretable on its own.

There are three useful scales for communicating this effect, each suited to a different audience.

Scale 1 — Odds ratio

Exponentiating the log-odds coefficient converts it to an odds ratio (OR):

exp(0.15) # point estimate → 1.16
[1] 1.161834
exp(-0.37) # lower 95% CI  → 0.69
[1] 0.6907343
exp(0.68) # upper 95% CI  → 1.97
[1] 1.973878

An OR of 1.16 means the odds of a correct response are 16% higher in the embodied group. ORs are standard in clinical and epidemiological reporting but still require some statistical literacy. Crucially, the 95% CI on the OR scale [0.69, 1.97] comfortably spans 1.0, reflecting the substantial uncertainty in this estimate.

Scale 2 — Probability scale

The most interpretable scale for a general audience is the probability of a correct response. Converting from log-odds to probability requires a baseline, which is provided by the model intercept (−0.17 at pre_score_z = 0, reference group = control). The conversion uses the inverse-logit function plogis():

plogis(-0.17) # control group baseline: ~0.46
[1] 0.4576021
plogis(-0.17 + 0.15) # embodied group:         ~0.50
[1] 0.4950002

The implied effect in plain language: at average pre-test score, the model estimates roughly 46% correct in control vs. 50% correct in embodied — a difference of approximately 4 percentage points (Δp ≈ 0.04).

Note that this calculation uses fixed-effect point estimates only and ignores varying effects, so it represents the population-average probability for a hypothetical average item and participant. Individual participants and items vary substantially around this value (see the varying effects SDs in summary(fit_b1)).

Scale 3 — Cohen’s h

For collaborators accustomed to thinking in terms of Cohen’s d, Cohen’s h is the natural standardised effect size for a difference between two proportions:

# h = 2 * arcsin(sqrt(p1)) - 2 * arcsin(sqrt(p2))
2 * asin(sqrt(0.50)) - 2 * asin(sqrt(0.46)) # → ~0.08
[1] 0.08008558

Conventional benchmarks for h: 0.2 = small, 0.5 = medium, 0.8 = large. The current point estimate (h ≈ 0.08) is well below the conventional small-effect threshold, consistent with the wide posterior credible interval.

From effect sizes to posterior inference

Once a simulated dataset has been generated and a model fitted, effect sizes are recovered on the probability scale using add_epred_draws(), rather than point estimates. This properly propagates both parameter uncertainty and the varying effects structure:

# newdata grid: one row per group, pre_score_z held at 0
nd <- tibble::tibble(
  group = c("control", "embodied"),
  pre_score_z = 0
)

# posterior over Δp for a single fitted model
delta_p_post <- b_sim |>
  tidybayes::add_epred_draws(newdata = nd) |>
  tidybayes::compare_levels(.epred, by = group) |>
  dplyr::summarise(
    median = median(.epred),
    q025 = quantile(.epred, 0.025),
    q975 = quantile(.epred, 0.975),
    width = q975 - q025
  )

Two notes on terminology worth clarifying for collaborators:

  • add_epred_draws() returns the posterior over the expected probability — i.e., plogis(η) for each draw — and is the right tool for estimating and comparing group-level probabilities. It does not return 0/1 outcomes.

  • posterior_predict() goes one step further, drawing a 0 or 1 from Bernoulli(p) for each draw. This adds observation-level noise on top of parameter uncertainty and is primarily useful for posterior predictive checks (e.g., pp_check()), not for effect size estimation.

Precision as the target, not just power

In a Bayesian simulation study, the most natural design target is posterior precision, rather than a binary powered/underpowered verdict. Across simulation iterations, we can summarise:

  • Directional accuracy: proportion of iterations where the posterior median is in the correct direction.
  • 95% CI exclusion: proportion of iterations where the 95% credible interval on Δp excludes zero (the NHST-adjacent criterion).
  • Interval width: mean width of the 95% CI on Δp across iterations — how precisely can we estimate the accuracy difference at a given N?

The last metric is arguably the most useful for design decisions: rather than asking “can we detect the effect?”, we ask “how precisely can we characterise it?”. For example, a width of ±5 percentage points on Δp means we can estimate the accuracy difference to within that margin, which is directly meaningful to a collaborator unfamiliar with null hypothesis testing.

5. Before we simulate: key questions and considerations

The previous section established how to read and translate the effect size estimates from model fit_b1. Before proceeding to formal simulations, it is worth pausing to ask a set of questions. This is not a mechanical step. It is a scientific one: the goal is to arrive at the simulation with a clear sense of what we are trying to learn and what design choices are still open.

The three questions below are not independent. They have a natural dependency structure, and working through them in order prevents a common error in study planning — optimising precision for an effect that may not be worth pursuing, or scaling up a design that could be restructured more efficiently. For some really helpful background context to effect sizes in psychological research more generally, I recommend first reading this paper by Funder and Ozer (Funder & Ozer, 2019).

5.1 Is the effect plausibly large enough to be worth pursuing?

This is the first and most important question, and it is one that pilot data with wide uncertainty can still inform to some degree. With this said, we should always keep in mind that if pilot data is based on a tiny fraction of a “typical” dataset, then it will suffer from sampling variability to a larger degree and thus provide over- or under-estimates of the true population value of interest.

The posterior estimate for the group effect in fit_b1 is β = 0.15 on the log-odds scale (95% CI: −0.37, 0.68), corresponding to Δp ≈ 4 percentage points at the estimated baseline. As the effect size table in 4. Understanding effect sizes under the logit link shows, even the upper bound of the credible interval (~16pp, h ≈ 0.32) would constitute only a small-to-moderate effect by conventional benchmarks. The lower bound is consistent with no effect or even a small negative effect.

This is not a reason to abandon the research question. But it does frame the simulation work that follows: we are not in a situation where the effect is clearly present and the only question is how much data we need to estimate it precisely. We are in a situation where we need to ask:

  • What is the minimum effect size that would be theoretically meaningful or practically relevant for this intervention? If a 4pp improvement in accuracy is too small to justify adoption, then we need a design sensitive enough to rule it out as well as detect it when present.
  • What does the wider literature on embodied learning suggest about plausible effect magnitudes? If comparable interventions typically produce effects in the 10–20pp range, the pilot estimate is consistent with that and the question shifts to precision. If the literature suggests larger effects, the pilot may be flagging a design problem rather than a small true effect.

The simulation grid in 6. Run some simulations varies β across the range established in 4. Power and precision are reported as a function of β or sample size, so the reader can evaluate their own substantive threshold on the resulting curves.

5.2 What does more data buy us, and how much more is feasible?

Assuming the effect is worth pursuing, the next question is whether more of the same design — more participants, same between-person structure — is a viable path to adequate precision.

Two features of the pilot data are relevant here.

First, the between-person variance is large: the varying intercept SD for participants is estimated at 1.03 on the log-odds scale. This is substantial. It means there is considerable variability in overall accuracy across individuals, and this variability is pure noise in a between-person comparison — it inflates the uncertainty around the group effect without contributing any information about it. Any between-person design is fighting against this variance.

Second, the item-level variance is also non-trivial (SD(item intercept) = 1.24), and there is a varying slope for group across items (SD = 0.19). This means the effect of the intervention varies somewhat across items, which adds a further source of uncertainty that does not disappear with more participants.

We can already form a rough expectation before simulating: increasing N within the current between-person design will reduce uncertainty in the group effect, but the rate of return will be modest because a large share of the residual variance is structural (between persons, between items) rather than reducible by adding participants alone. The simulations in 6. Run some simulations quantify this explicitly, but the pilot data already suggest we should think carefully about whether there is a more efficient design available (see 5.3 Does the design itself need to change? below).

A practical consideration also applies here: what sample sizes are actually feasible? The current dataset has 108 participants. Doubling to ~200 may be straightforward; scaling to 500+ may not be. The simulation section reports precision as a function of N across a range that spans plausible sample size, so collaborators can make an informed judgement about whether the precision gain at a given N justifies the recruitment cost.

5.3 Does the design itself need to change?

This is often the highest-leverage question, and one that is easy to overlook when the default assumption is that a larger version of the current design is the natural next step.

The current design is between-person: each participant is assigned to either the control or embodied condition, contributing data to one group only. The large between-person variance identified above (SD ≈ 1.03) is a direct cost of this design choice: individual differences in general accuracy are confounded with the group comparison, and the only way to separate them is through random assignment — which works in expectation but leaves substantial residual uncertainty at any finite N.

A within-person (crossover) design, in which each participant experiences both conditions (on different items or at different time points), would partition out this between-person variance entirely. The “group” comparison would then be estimated from within-person differences, removing the largest single source of noise in the current model. In practical terms, this is often equivalent to running a study with substantially more participants for the same data collection effort — though it introduces its own considerations (order effects, fatigue, item overlap). And for many research designs in psychology and education research, it is simply not possible to use a within-person design.

6. Run some simulations

6.1 background sources

The basic structure and code follows the examples outlined by Solomon Kurz in his ‘power’ blog posts, and Lisa Debruine’s R package faux{} (DeBruine & Barr, 2021). The faux package vignettes are particularly useful and worthwhile working through.

You could also look at these examples by Rich, which show how you might simulate multi-level data for a range of factorial designs: https://github.com/rich-ramsey/sim_demo.

6.2 general approach

The basic workflow is as follows:

  1. Fit a regression model to data (real or simulated).
  2. Use the regression model parameters to simulate new data.
  3. Write a function to create 100s or 1000s of datasets and vary parameters of interest (e.g., sample size, trial count, effect size).
  4. Summarise the simulated data to estimate likely power or precision of the various research designs.

6.3 simulate one dataset

a simplified example to show the process

Let’s just pretend that this is a single level model (no varying effects) with two groups and one testing time. Maybe not a sensible design, but useful for thinking about simulating data. There are just two fixed effects. b0 - intercept (control group) and b1 (embodied group estimate).

The point here is just to see that a “fixed effect only” model has the same probability estimate for all participants (dots in the plot) and all items (facets in the plot). That is nothing new, it merely shows what a fixed-effects only model is doing. And it makes clear the relatively unrealistic assumptions that that kind of model entails. It is not that these models cannot be useful, just that when you study human beings, as well as a variety of different stimuli/items, they are likely to be more variable. And for this kind of fixed-effects only model, if you are ok with using frequentist stats, then a programme like G*Power would be fine to give you a ballpark estimate of power. But in most cases, a more complex model would seem better suited to studying human behaviour (Barr et al., 2013; McElreath, 2020).

sim <- function(
  subj_n = 100,
  item_n = 8,
  # fixed effects
  b0 = -0.17,
  b1 = 0.15,
  b2 = 0.31,
  # varying effects by items

  u1i_sd = 0.19,
  # varying effects by subjects
  u0s_sd = 1.03,
  # cors
  r01i = -0.11, # correlation between varying effects 0 and 1 for items
  ... # helps the function work with pmap() below
) {

  data <- add_random(subj = subj_n, item = item_n) |>
    add_between("subj", group = c("control", "embodied")) |>

    mutate(prob = plogis(y)) |>

Based on my conversations with various people in the learning and instruction lab, as well as many folks in other psychology and human neuroscience labs, there is a discrepancy between how people say they plan studies (or how they would like to plan studies) and how they plan studies in practice. 
Most people try to concoct an *a-prior* power analysis, since that seems to be deemed more acceptable in mainstream journals and general discourse. 
But scratch beneath the surface and the most common approach *in practice* is the "resource constraints" approach. 
This is not a criticism, it is simply an observation.

The resource constraints approach targets the maximum reasonable sample size possible based on the resources available. 
This resource-based approach lends itself to planning via determining the minimal statistically detectable effect, or a sensitivity power analysis (rather than an *a-priori* power analysis; see @tbl-lakens-evaluation). 
Using resource constraints to determine effect size sensitivity would be a lot simpler than the simulation-based approaches outlined below, as it determines the size of an effect that one could detect with a specified research design and level of statistical power.
So, before you get lost digging into the weeds below, I would strongly suggest that you have a conversation with your research group about implications of a resource constraints approach within the context of your project.
A resource constraints approach will save you a lot of time, and in the majority of cases it will more straightforwardly embody how your sample size is being set in practice.

And for reasonably simple designs and analyses that avoid more complex, multi-level modelling approaches, then a tool like [G*Power](https://www.psychologie.hhu.de/arbeitsgruppen/allgemeine-psychologie-und-arbeitspsychologie/gpower) can be very useful.
And even if you plan on fitting a multi-level model, G\*Power can be useful to give you a quick-and-dirty ballpark estimate based on a simplified aspect of your research design.
So, I would recommend becoming familiar with G\*Power.

But, if you want to plan a study that does try to capture the nested or multi-level nature of the data, such as by allowing for variation across participants or measurement items [@barrRandomEffectsStructure2013], then things get complicated fast.
And we need to harness the power of data simulation.
So, if you're a sucker for punishment, and if you want a more involved and simulated-based approach, then continue reading. 

The approach is primarily based on two resources: a series of [blog posts](https://solomonkurz.netlify.app/tags/power/) by Solomon Kurz, and an R package called [{faux}](https://www.scienceverse.org/faux/) by Lisa Debruine. 
There are also many other simulation-based approaches to plan studies with multi-level models - see the bottom of this [link](https://www.scienceverse.org/faux/) for details. 
And more generally, I would strongly recommend using data simulation more generally for a variety of reasons throughout the scientific workflow that extend beyond planning [@gelman2024before; @debruineUnderstandingMixedEffectsModels2021]. 
See this [talk](https://debruine.github.io/talks/EMPSEB-fake-it-2023/#/title-slide) by Lisa DeBruine for a nice summary.

The basic approach that I follow here is as follows:

1. Find existing data.
2. Fit a model to the data.
3. Use the model to guide expectations on multi-level structure.
4. Use simulations to vary parameters and guide sample size decisions.

Before running simulations, it is worth establishing a clear mapping between the model's native parameterisation and effect sizes that are interpretable to a general audience. 
This section documents that mapping as a reference point for collaborators and for later simulation design.

## The scale problem

Model `fit_b1` uses a Bernoulli likelihood with the logit link, so all regression coefficients live on the **log-odds scale**. 
The posterior estimate for `groupembodied` is 0.15 (95% CI: −0.37, 0.68). 
This means that being in the embodied group increases the log-odds of a correct response by 0.15 relative to control — a number that is essentially uninterpretable on its own.

There are three useful scales for communicating this effect, each suited to a different audience.

### Scale 1 — Odds ratio

Exponentiating the log-odds coefficient converts it to an **odds ratio** (OR):

```{r}
#| label: odds-ratio
#| code-fold: false
exp(0.15) # point estimate → 1.16
exp(-0.37) # lower 95% CI  → 0.69
exp(0.68) # upper 95% CI  → 1.97
```

An OR of 1.16 means the odds of a correct response are 16% higher in the embodied group. 
ORs are standard in clinical and epidemiological reporting but still require some statistical literacy. 
Crucially, the 95% CI on the OR scale [0.69, 1.97] comfortably spans 1.0, reflecting the substantial uncertainty in this estimate.

### Scale 2 — Probability scale

The most interpretable scale for a general audience is the **probability** of a correct response. 
Converting from log-odds to probability requires a baseline, which is provided by the model intercept (−0.17 at `pre_score_z = 0`, reference group = control). 
The conversion uses the inverse-logit function `plogis()`:

```{r}
#| label: prob-scale
#| code-fold: false
plogis(-0.17) # control group baseline: ~0.46
plogis(-0.17 + 0.15) # embodied group:         ~0.50
```

The implied effect in plain language: at average pre-test score, the model estimates roughly **46% correct in control vs. 50% correct in embodied** — a difference of approximately 4 percentage points (Δ*p* ≈ 0.04).

Note that this calculation uses fixed-effect point estimates only and ignores varying effects, so it represents the population-average probability for a hypothetical average item and participant. 
Individual participants and items vary substantially around this value (see the varying effects SDs in `summary(fit_b1)`).

### Scale 3 — Cohen's *h*

For collaborators accustomed to thinking in terms of Cohen's *d*, **Cohen's h** is the natural standardised effect size for a difference between two proportions:

```{r}
#| label: cohens-h
#| code-fold: false
# h = 2 * arcsin(sqrt(p1)) - 2 * arcsin(sqrt(p2))
2 * asin(sqrt(0.50)) - 2 * asin(sqrt(0.46)) # → ~0.08
```

Conventional benchmarks for *h*: 0.2 = small, 0.5 = medium, 0.8 = large. 
The current point estimate (*h* ≈ 0.08) is well below the conventional small-effect threshold, consistent with the wide posterior credible interval.

## From effect sizes to posterior inference

Once a simulated dataset has been generated and a model fitted, effect sizes are recovered on the probability scale using `add_epred_draws()`, rather than point estimates. 
This properly propagates both parameter uncertainty and the varying effects structure:

```{r}
#| label: epred-workflow
#| eval: false
#| code-fold: false
# newdata grid: one row per group, pre_score_z held at 0
nd <- tibble::tibble(
  group = c("control", "embodied"),
  pre_score_z = 0
)

# posterior over Δp for a single fitted model
delta_p_post <- b_sim |>
  tidybayes::add_epred_draws(newdata = nd) |>
  tidybayes::compare_levels(.epred, by = group) |>
  dplyr::summarise(
    median = median(.epred),
    q025 = quantile(.epred, 0.025),
    q975 = quantile(.epred, 0.975),
    width = q975 - q025
  )
```

Two notes on terminology worth clarifying for collaborators:

- **`add_epred_draws()`** returns the posterior over the *expected probability*
  — i.e., `plogis(η)` for each draw — and is the right tool for estimating and comparing group-level probabilities. It does *not* return 0/1 outcomes.

- **`posterior_predict()`** goes one step further, drawing a 0 or 1 from `Bernoulli(p)` for each draw. This adds observation-level noise on top of parameter uncertainty and is primarily useful for posterior predictive checks (e.g., `pp_check()`), not for effect size estimation.

### Precision as the target, not just power

In a Bayesian simulation study, the most natural design target is **posterior precision**, rather than a binary powered/underpowered verdict. 
Across simulation iterations, we can summarise:

- **Directional accuracy**: proportion of iterations where the posterior median is in the correct direction.
- **95% CI exclusion**: proportion of iterations where the 95% credible interval on Δ*p* excludes zero (the NHST-adjacent criterion).
- **Interval width**: mean width of the 95% CI on Δ*p* across iterations — how precisely can we estimate the accuracy difference at a given *N*?

The last metric is arguably the most useful for design decisions: rather than asking "can we detect the effect?", we ask "how precisely can we characterise it?". 
For example, a width of ±5 percentage points on Δ*p* means we can estimate the accuracy difference to within that margin, which is directly meaningful to a collaborator unfamiliar with null hypothesis testing.

</details>

# 5. Before we simulate: key questions and considerations

The previous section established how to read and translate the effect size estimates from model `fit_b1`. 
Before proceeding to formal simulations, it is worth pausing to ask a set of questions. 
This is not a mechanical step. 
It is a scientific one: the goal is to arrive at the simulation with a clear sense of *what we are trying to learn* and *what design choices are still open*.

The three questions below are not independent. 
They have a natural dependency structure, and working through them in order prevents a common error in study planning — optimising precision for an effect that may not be worth pursuing, or scaling up a design that could be restructured more efficiently.
For some really helpful background context to effect sizes in psychological research more generally, I recommend first reading this paper by Funder and Ozer [@funderEvaluatingEffectSize2019].

---

## 5.1 Is the effect plausibly large enough to be worth pursuing?

This is the first and most important question, and it is one that pilot data with wide uncertainty can still inform to some degree.
With this said, we should always keep in mind that if pilot data is based on a tiny fraction of a "typical" dataset, then it will suffer from sampling variability to a larger degree and thus provide over- or under-estimates of the true population value of interest. 

The posterior estimate for the group effect in `fit_b1` is β = 0.15 on the log-odds scale (95% CI: −0.37, 0.68), corresponding to Δ*p* ≈ 4 percentage points at the estimated baseline. 
As the effect size table in @sec-effect-sizes shows, even the upper bound of the credible interval (~16pp, *h* ≈ 0.32) would constitute only a small-to-moderate effect by conventional benchmarks. 
The lower bound is consistent with no effect or even a small negative effect.

This is not a reason to abandon the research question. 
But it does frame the simulation work that follows: we are not in a situation where the effect is clearly present and the only question is how much data we need to estimate it precisely. 
We are in a situation where we need to ask:

- What is the **minimum effect size that would be theoretically meaningful** or
  practically relevant for this intervention? If a 4pp improvement in accuracy
  is too small to justify adoption, then we need a design sensitive enough to
  rule it *out* as well as detect it when present.
- What does the **wider literature** on embodied learning suggest about plausible
  effect magnitudes? If comparable interventions typically produce effects in the
  10–20pp range, the pilot estimate is consistent with that and the question
  shifts to precision. If the literature suggests larger effects, the pilot may
  be flagging a design problem rather than a small true effect.

The simulation grid in @sec-sims varies β across the range established in @sec-effect-sizes precisely to avoid committing to a single assumed effect size.
Power and precision are reported as a function of β or sample size, so the reader can evaluate their own substantive threshold on the resulting curves.

---

## 5.2 What does more data buy us, and how much more is feasible?

Assuming the effect is worth pursuing, the next question is whether *more of the same design* — more participants, same between-person structure — is a viable path to adequate precision.

Two features of the pilot data are relevant here.

First, the **between-person variance is large**: the varying intercept SD for participants is estimated at 1.03 on the log-odds scale. 
This is substantial.
It means there is considerable variability in overall accuracy across individuals, and this variability is pure noise in a between-person comparison — it inflates the uncertainty around the group effect without contributing any information about it. 
Any between-person design is fighting against this variance.

Second, the **item-level variance is also non-trivial** (SD(item intercept) = 1.24), and there is a varying slope for group across items (SD = 0.19). 
This means the effect of the intervention varies somewhat across items, which adds a further source of uncertainty that does not disappear with more participants.

We can already form a rough expectation before simulating: increasing N within the current between-person design will reduce uncertainty in the group effect, but the rate of return will be modest because a large share of the residual variance is structural (between persons, between items) rather than reducible by adding participants alone. 
The simulations in @sec-sims quantify this explicitly, but the pilot data already suggest we should think carefully about whether there is a more efficient design available (see @sec-design-change below).

A practical consideration also applies here: what sample sizes are actually feasible? 
The current dataset has 108 participants. 
Doubling to ~200 may be straightforward; scaling to 500+ may not be. 
The simulation section reports precision as a function of N across a range that spans plausible sample size, so collaborators can make an informed judgement about whether the precision gain at a given N justifies the recruitment cost.

---

## 5.3 Does the design itself need to change? {#sec-design-change}

This is often the highest-leverage question, and one that is easy to overlook when the default assumption is that a larger version of the current design is the natural next step.

The current design is **between-person**: each participant is assigned to either the control or embodied condition, contributing data to one group only. 
The large between-person variance identified above (SD ≈ 1.03) is a direct cost of this design choice: individual differences in general accuracy are confounded with the group comparison, and the only way to separate them is through random assignment — which works in expectation but leaves substantial residual uncertainty at any finite N.

A **within-person (crossover) design**, in which each participant experiences both conditions (on different items or at different time points), would partition out this between-person variance entirely. 
The "group" comparison would then be estimated from within-person differences, removing the largest single source of noise in the current model. 
In practical terms, this is often equivalent to running a study with substantially more participants for the same data collection effort — though it introduces its own considerations (order effects, fatigue, item overlap).
And for many research designs in psychology and education research, it is simply not possible to use a within-person design. 

---

The three questions above have a natural ordering that structures the simulation work that follows:

## 6.2 general approach ##

The basic workflow is as follows:

1. Fit a regression model to data (real or simulated).
2. Use the regression model parameters to simulate new data.
3. Write a function to create 100s or 1000s of datasets and vary parameters of interest (e.g., sample size, trial count, effect size).
4. Summarise the simulated data to estimate likely power or precision of the various research designs.

sim <- function(
  subj_n = 100,
  item_n = 8,
  # fixed effects
  b0 = -0.17,
  b1 = 0.15,
  b2 = 0.31,
  # varying effects by items
  u0i_sd = 1.24,
  u1i_sd = 0.19,
  # varying effects by subjects
  u0s_sd = 1.03,
  # cors
  r01i = -0.11, # correlation between varying effects 0 and 1 for items
  ... # helps the function work with pmap() below
) {
  # set up data structure
  data <- add_random(subj = subj_n, item = item_n) |>
    add_between("subj", group = c("control", "embodied")) |>
    add_contrast(
      "group",
      "treatment",
      add_cols = TRUE,
      colnames = "embodied"
    ) |>
    add_ranef("subj", u0s = u0s_sd, pre_score_z = 1, .cors = 0) |>
    add_ranef("item", u0i = u0i_sd, u1i = u1i_sd, .cors = r01i) |>
    ## calculate DV in log odds
    mutate(
      y = b0 + u0s + u0i + (b1 + u1i) * embodied + b2 * pre_score_z
    ) |>
    ## calculate DV in log odds
    mutate(prob = plogis(y)) |>
    ## calculate outcome
    mutate(correct = rbinom(n(), size = 1, prob = prob)) |>
    ## rename to match fit_b1 column names
    rename(pid = subj, item_id = item)
  #glimpse(data) # only use this when testing the code
}

Instead of focusing on rejecting a null hypothesis, we might instead use our simulation skills to determine the sample size we need to have most of our 95% intervals come in at a certain level of precision. 
This has been termed the accuracy in parameter estimation [AIPE; Maxwell et al.(2008); see also Kruschke (2015)] approach to sample size planning.

Thinking in terms of AIPE, in terms of precision, let’s say we wanted widths of 1.2, 1.0, 0.8 or smaller. 
These are just arbitrary values based on the output of the sims so far. 
For instance, there is no inherent meaning to these widths outside of the research context and typical precision that is expected.
Here’s how we did with our sims.

```{r}
#| label: calc-precision
#| code-fold: false
precision <- lower_q |>
  mutate(
    below_12 = if_else(width < 1.2, 1, 0),
    below_10 = if_else(width < 1.0, 1, 0),
    below_08 = if_else(width < 0.8, 1, 0)
  ) |>
  summarise(
    power_12 = mean(below_12),
    power_10 = mean(below_10),
    power_08 = mean(below_08),
    .by = c(b1, subj_n)
  )
precision
#write_csv(precision, "data/precision.csv")
```

ok, at N=150 per group (or higher), widths < 1.2 look good. 1.0 and .8 look much worse.

This simulation suggests we have a high probability of achieving 95% CI widths of 1.2 or smaller with n=150 or higher.
